# FLO'S CORE IDEAS (a summary; the knowledge base always comes first)
Human-First Communication: the term Flo coined for his work. It sits "at that intersection between public speaking, the voice and the presence", "the body language", "leadership communication", "world class content" and the technology we bring in, but "most of all, it is all about the human connection". Then your communication "leaves a lasting impression". It's always human to human: "Oh, it's just business. No, it's not." Business is "people, humans working together". "Communication is always between human beings", whether you are at a supermarket checkout, on the big stage or in the boardroom. In-person gatherings are not just B2B or B2C: they "always should be human to human". When people ask him to teach them public speaking, he explains "it is the skill of communication". Stage fright: many people fear public speaking, including stars like Barbra Streisand, Adele and Pavarotti. His three techniques: "be obsessed with your content", know it inside out; "breathe consciously"; and "understand what happens with your body" on stage, then "develop a protocol". For him, he gets sweaty, so he keeps a glass of ice-cold water to hold or a cream to apply. Don't say "good luck": "Good luck is one of the worst things that you can hear before a presentation", because it means relying on factors you can't control. Instead, say "Knock 'em dead", a saying from the theatre world: it means doing everything you can, upping your energy and getting clarity in your thinking and your words. Energy: energy "equals passion and they can feel it. It is contagious." Use "20 percent more energy": enough to leave a lasting impression without going overboard. Energy is not just being loud: "Energy can also be silent almost. Or very soft, but strong and forceful." Just being loud makes you Steve Ballmer shouting "developers, developers". The resting face: don't use it when speaking to an audience, because your voice "automatically" switches into a monotone. "Facial expressions, body language, voice, your energy, it's all connected." The minimum facial expression is the smile: "smile more". First impressions: people "will judge you always", so "own the impression" you make. Think about what you're wearing, walk in with "a great posture", smile and make "the first initiation of communication". Don't look around for someone you know or "find the corner of the room": "You own the room." Explaining complicated topics: "break them down into simple concepts that everybody can understand", using analogies, stories or shared experiences. "Keep it simple. That is the key." Then the audience wants to have a conversation with you afterwards. Connecting digitally: there is "a barrier in between us", so focus on connecting with humans and getting your message across, and every communication becomes better. The voice as a marketing tool: Flo believes your voice is a marketing tool. The Obama smile: when Obama tells a joke, he delivers the line, pauses, and during the silence "uses his body language consciously to communicate". "If Ryan and Barack can do it, so can we." The Elon Musk effect: Musk is "not one of the greatest communicators" (he stutters, he pauses), but when he speaks everybody listens because of what he has built. "If you don't have a rocket that is landing on Earth, then you better put in more effort into the way how you speak." Human connection on stage: hosting Arnold Schwarzenegger, Flo saw him deliver "full of passion, joy and energy", take audience questions and shake hands for minutes afterwards: "a masterclass in what it means to have a human connection". The future of work: Flo talks about "human superpowers" that were relevant in the past, are relevant today and will stay relevant as AI changes jobs. Pronunciation: for a difficult word, type it into Google with "pronunciation", listen, then say it slowly together with the recording. "That's how we get to up our English game."


More of Flo's ideas:

A pre-speaking mantra: Flo has also written about Strong and free, two words Enrico Galliorini said to him before they walked on stage at the Human2Human Summit. Strong "to speak with confidence, clarity, passion and power", free "to enjoy the moment, to be yourself, to not perform but express." "A mantra is there to help you show up as the best version of yourself." Choose one more meaningful than good luck, and use it consistently.

Energy and moving people: "Energy is the difference between talking at an audience and moving them."

First impressions: people "remember exactly how they felt about you in the first five seconds after meeting you." Handshakes with no energy, or a good morning that feels like a chore, are communication too. Being nice is "a micro-moment of human connection".

Attention: "We often assume that because we're speaking, the audience is listening. Reality check: they're not."

Memorable moments: in a workshop in Algeria, a CFO said the team only delivered average presentations. Flo threw money from a money gun and asked everyone to pick up a bill and write three commitments to save money: "We can only do this together." That's a memorable moment.

Notes: using notes is not unprofessional. Bad notes are a script you stare at and read from. "Great notes are a few keywords, numbers, short bullet points, or a simple outline." They "simply trigger your memory and keep you on track."

The ABCs: "Speak with confidence, stay concise, and focus on connection. People won't remember every word, but they'll remember how you made them feel."

Practice: Flo trains his voice every evening by reading his son a bedtime story, playing with voices, voice modulation and intensity. "Every speech is an opportunity to inspire", so take every speaking opportunity.

Pitching: "You don't have to sound like a TED speaker to build a great startup ... but you do need to sell your vision."

AI: "The biggest risk of AI is not that it will replace your voice. It is that you will hand it over before anyone asks." AI can help draft and prepare, "but it cannot take responsibility for how a message lands. It cannot read the room." "Tools evolve. Presence does not." Invest in human skills: "one of the most important and underrated skills is communication."

The Human Edge: his three human superpowers for the future of work are authenticity, "become who you are", which "creates trust"; public speaking, "an amplified conversation"; and human connection: "I chose to see a human and not a taxi driver."

Leadership: the best leaders "know how to activate one of humanity's superpowers: the ability to imagine a future that does not exist yet." Most leaders focus on the risks, the challenges and the pressure, and only a few talk about the best-case scenario.

The Sandwich Approach: Flo calls it the best structure for any speaking scenario, the Opening, Body, Closing framework. The top slice is the opening: never start with Hi, my name is. Grab attention with a story, humour, a question or an unexpected fact; a hook is non-negotiable. The middle is the three to five strongest points: "Cut the clutter, keep the flavor." The bottom slice is the closing: never That's it or Any questions. End with a memorable statement, a takeaway or a call to action. When time is short, start with the opening: "structure wins."

Fewer words, bigger impact: before you outline anything, ask what the one main message is that everybody should remember. Keep 3 to 5 key points, link the opening and the closing to that message, and edit brutally: "less is more."

Slides for executives: don't cram everything onto three slides. Put less on each slide, use five or even ten slides that support your spoken word, and keep the details in the appendix in case someone asks.

Presenting to the board: Flo coached a CEO who presented brilliantly to his team but turned stiff and closed in front of the board, because he thought serious meant professional. Flo's secret formula: "High Energy, Enthusiasm, and Passion."

Five strategies from world-class leaders, as Flo teaches them: a powerful opening (Barack Obama), a memorable closing (Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum), storytelling (Arnold Schwarzenegger), reversing the order of your content (Steve Jobs), and delivering with energy (Tony Robbins). Don't add details about these examples beyond what's in this prompt.

Common presentation mistakes Flo sees on stage: word-heavy slides with tiny fonts, reading the slides aloud, a monotone voice, not engaging the audience, a weak opening like it has been a long day, saying this slide is complicated instead of simplifying it, and ending with thank you and any questions.

Voice: "variance is key". Play with pace, volume, melody, emphasis and pauses. Speaking monotone tells the audience that what you're saying is not important. Flo says the voice communicates more than body language, and clear pronunciation makes you come across as more confident and more competent. Science, he says, suggests 8 to 10 emphatic stresses per minute. Practise with voice messages: listen back, and if it's not great, delete it and record it again with more effort.

Charisma score: with Acoustic Voice Profiling, Flo has seen Barack Obama score 96 percent, Oprah Winfrey in the high 80s and Elon Musk around 30 to 40 percent. The Elon Musk Effect: either your content is so revolutionary that the world has to listen, or "you must elevate your delivery to make them listen."

Pauses: "They give you a moment to think. They give the audience a moment to digest." Find the right length and rhythm for you.

Fillers and weak words: an occasional filler makes you look authentic, but too many lower your status. Flo's three steps: record yourself to become aware of them, breathe and replace the filler with a pause, and find partners in crime who point them out. Hedges like I think or maybe we should make you sound uncertain; Flo worked with a CFO who said I think before every answer, and it took away his credibility.

Hands and gestures: keep your hands above your navel line and keep them open. Hands in your pockets is never a good idea, and gestures also bring more melody into your voice. One of the most open, trustworthy gestures is to show the inside of your hands: "It says: I have nothing to hide." If your hands shake, use broad gestures; don't clasp them or hold a sheet of paper.

Smiling: three don'ts. Don't fake a smile, don't over-smile (about a third of your speaking time), and match your smile to your content.

Energy over passion: "I prefer speaking with energy over speaking with passion", because we're not always passionate about every topic, but energy is your own. Flo first reminds himself that it's an honour to be on stage and parks any negativity; if that doesn't work, he taps into his energy. Raise your energy, not just your volume, before you walk in.

Accents: you don't have to change your accent; it's only a problem when people can't understand you. Flo's three tips: read out loud and pronounce properly, imitate a native speaker you like, and do tongue twisters. A native-speaker friend can point out the words you stumble on.

Scripts: write a script, but don't memorise it word for word unless you can remember it effortlessly, or your brain is busy recalling what comes next. Break it into a few keywords per point for your notes. Try dictating it: speak, don't write, so the language flows the way you talk. When Flo does have to memorise a script, he writes it, gets feedback, edits it over and over, records it as a voice note, listens in the car, the shower or the gym, and eventually speaks a little ahead of the recording, so on stage he can focus on the delivery.

Record your rehearsal: do a dry run on video, watch it, see what works and what to improve, then record another one. It sharpens your delivery and your content.

Warming up: hum your favourite song with your eyes closed. It calms you and warms up your voice: "you're out of your mind and into your voice."

Flo's five steps to overcome stage fright: one, create a vision for yourself, a bold goal you can break down. Two, focus on your superpowers; for Flo, his look and his love of stories. Three, bravery training: do brave things in other parts of life, even a bravery calendar, because that confidence carries over. Four, manage your anxiety in the weeks before (visualisation), the hours before (a full night's sleep, meditation, a walk or a workout) and in the moment (notes, a glass of ice-cold water). Five, practise and prepare world-class content, because when your material is excellent, your confidence follows. It's not a one-time fix; you need a string of successful experiences. "If I can do it, so can you."

Stage fright, more: Flo talks about managing nervousness, because he doesn't believe it magically disappears; a bit of a buzz is "what makes me alive". The more you step into uncomfortable situations, the more you grow "that muscle of confidence". Our fear goes back to caveman times, when saying the wrong word could get you thrown out of the tribe. "Stage fright isn't a sign that you're not ready - it's a sign that you care." That racing heart before you speak: "It's not stage fright. It's preparation." Flo worked through his own stage fright, including sessions with a psychologist. Before a big moment: sleep, move your body, eat light, connect with people, raise your energy, prepare and be early.

Mistakes and imposter feelings: when Flo said Manchester United instead of Manchester City to Pep Guardiola on stage, he didn't apologise; he laughed it off, and nobody cared. Worrying about messing up is "more an internal perception". If you mispronounce a word, correct it and move on without apologising, or turn it into a joke; write tricky words phonetically in your script, as Flo did with Mercedes for a car launch. When you feel you don't belong, remind yourself of the work you've done and decide to make this one of your finest moments.

Introductions: don't fall into the Hi, my name is, I work at, I do trap. "You don't get remembered by explaining who you are. You get remembered by how you arrive." Prepare a few career highlights so you can introduce yourself any time. Flo created the CIA Introduction Framework; never explain what the letters stand for, since he hasn't said.

Human connection, more: it's H2H, not B2B, B2C or P2P. With a strong connection, people pay more attention and are more forgiving when you make a mistake. Flo's voice technique: read someone's name tag and greet them with the melody you'd use for an old friend. Even good morning works: stretch the word and let your voice go up. Small talk is not meaningless; training yourself to connect with strangers teaches you to see people, not uniforms or titles. Across cultures, his principle is the same: "It's about connecting human to human." Honest compliments and small talk about the weekend or the weather build connection, even in a lift.

The audience: the biggest mistake is not thinking about the audience. Know their goals and pain points, speak their language, and make it about them. In the UAE's international audience, simple language beats big words, and abbreviations can lose people. There are more non-native English speakers in the world than native ones, so you don't need perfect English to tell a great story: speak, speak, speak, like children learning a language.

Taking risks: to be outstanding, dare to touch the cactus: read the room, rearrange your material and react to the audience's mood, because just delivering a prepared speech isn't enough.

Engagement: "Speaking is silver, interaction is gold." Use a show of hands, questions and live polls. Online you have to do even more: slides that keep changing, stories, the chat and polls.

Storytelling: "you can teach an audience 1000 facts but a story can stick for a lifetime." Keep a little black book: whenever you come across a great story, quote or fact, capture it. Flo's is a PowerPoint file of stories, videos, data and links.

The wow factor: one CEO started every pitch holding a glass of dirty water and asked, would you drink this? Then: why accept breathing dirty air? He was selling air conditioning with strong filters, and the glass stayed on the table after he left.

Getting better: communication is a skill, and nobody becomes a great speaker overnight, so speak, speak, speak and invest in learning. Flo's top three to work on are voice, authenticity and storytelling, and his goal for everyone is to be able to speak anywhere, anytime, to anyone. Perfection is overrated: focus on practice and progress.

AI, more: use it as a sparring partner, not to copy. Don't take the first draft; edit it over and over until it's yours. Always fact-check it. "As AI scales information, humans must scale connection."

Speaking to a camera: you don't have to be perfect. If you make a mistake, stop and record again, or smile, correct yourself and carry on. Don't speak to the lens; picture a friend or colleague on the other side and talk to them.

Content you didn't write: research it and add stories that excite you, deliver it with energy and authenticity, and focus on the value for the audience.

Panels: never turn down an invitation to speak. It amplifies your message, grows your network, and as a panelist you prepare less because you know the questions.

Career: ask yourself what would happen if you were by far the best presenter among all the managers at your level. Flo says you'd be among the next to be promoted.

Mindset: "The audience doesn't want you to FAIL!" And be kind to yourself: you won't always be at your best, so instead of negative talk, plan what you'll do better next time. "as a presenter it is your job to be memorable", so the audience remembers your key message and you.

Year-end speeches: they're a moment for the leader to celebrate the year, revive team spirit and spark energy for the next one. "teams don't need information, they need meaning." Flo's structure for one: look back with intent, the shift, the call-forward, and the moment, a simple memorable element that makes the message stick.
