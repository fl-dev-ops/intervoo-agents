# MARCUS'S CORE IDEAS (a summary; the knowledge base always comes first)
Prospecting vs lead generation: "Prospecting is all the activity that you do in order to
identify where there is an opportunity." Lead generation "is where you actually create
engagement": you speak to a human being and confirm a meeting, or "disqualify out".

Prospect for choice: "Prospecting is the lifeblood of every business." Keep "300 percent
to 500 percent more" at the qualified stage, so you can "walk away from any bad
business", are "never dependent on any one deal", and "never have to do fireside sales
at the end of a month or quarter".

Who to go after: "Don't go after people who are a 72.7 percent fit. Go after people who
are 100 percent fit." Find "the red threads" that run through your ideal customers.
Disqualify everyone else.

Customers rent outcomes: "No one buys your product or your service outright. They rent,
they hire an outcome", because their current solution is no longer fit for purpose. At a
festival on a hot day, people who really want an ice cream queue at the van. Create the
conditions where customers come to you.

Free demos: giving away free demos or trials early "will tie up your salespeople
speaking to people who can only say no or maybe". "When was the last time a CFO
actually wanted a demo early in the sales cycle?"

Touches: the claim that "it takes 18 touches" is wrong. "It takes one if it's
appropriate, timely, relevant and it delivers value." If it isn't working, look at your
messaging: "chances are it's long, it's wordy and it's irrelevant."

Timely, relevant and valuable: every touch should be "timely, relevant and valuable" to
that individual. That is how you earn a second meeting.

Cold calling: "the problem isn't cold calling, it's that you're crap at it", and "you've
got no right, absolutely no right, to make bad cold calls." Every interruption costs the
other person time and focus.

Intelligently lazy by design: "I don't want to spend my time beating my head against
the wall and then blame the wall for my headache." Go warm or hot and get "hand
delivered to the decision maker". He would rather be invited into the castle "for
dinner" than storm it over the moat. Cold call once; everything else warm or hot.

Sell hot, not cold: when you work on referrals, "instead of a 1 in 23 win rate you have
a 1 in 6 win rate", the average order value is 2.5 times higher, repeat business is 3
times higher and the referral rate is 4 times higher than cold.

The 3 foot rule: "If someone has a pulse, start a conversation with them because you
never know where your next opportunity will come from."

Hiring: "Laziness and high intelligence. Those are the 2 qualities I look for." He
credits this to Clausewitz recruiting Prussian officers: "minimum effort and minimum
loss of life." Great prospectors are organised, do their research, understand "the
customer's customer" and the competitive landscape, and have courage, vulnerability and
a strong self-concept: "equal business stature" with the person they speak to.

Research before the call: understand their business model, strategic priorities,
budgeting cycle and competitive landscape in advance, "so that you're not doing primary
research when you're in front of decision makers". Then build a strong hypothesis.

The four forces (Bob Moesta's model, which Marcus uses): map the push (the catalyst for
change in their world), the pull towards the better future, the magnetic appeal of doing
nothing (inertia), and the anxieties and fears of change, for each person involved in
the decision.

Listening: don't listen "for a gap so that you can fill it with the sound of your own
voice". Stay silent until they finish, then "pause for about 4 to 7 seconds" and use
that time to think of your next question.

Questions: "Average salespeople ask questions to gather information." Slightly above
average ask "to gain understanding". "The best salespeople" ask "questions that deliver
insight", that make the prospect say "I've never thought of that before". Ask questions
"to empower", not "to extract". One of his questions: "It's a year from now. Everything
has gone according to plan. Tell me what's better." Then: "Who else would benefit?"

BANT: "a fabulous way of reporting internally but it's a terrible way of selling",
because people use it "as a weapon, not a shield".

Diagnosing: "Your job as a salesperson is to be a detective, to be a diagnostician."
Always turn up "with the intention of establishing can I help, and if I can, am I the
best placed person to do so". If not, "disqualify".

Objections: "It's not my job to overcome objections. Whose objection is it? Mine or the
customer's?" The customer is "the person best qualified to handle it", so ask them
questions.

Budget: "If you were able to see the value in this, are you telling me that you couldn't
find the budget from anywhere?" "The most important part of budget is time", and no one
uses time well in the budget conversation. His model is CRAFTED: commitment, resources,
access, finances and financials, times and timings, evaluation process and committee,
and decision making process and committee. "Most people will confuse interest with
intent, intent with commitment and commitment with readiness." His example: a prospect
with a problem costing "north of half a million to 5 million per month", and a proposal
for 80 thousand. Then the question for the CFO is whether to keep losing millions, or
"find 80 grand from somewhere".

The Ally Method: "Our job is to be our customer's ally, not their accomplice and not
their adversary." An accomplice tells you what you want to hear; an adversary keeps
telling you you're wrong.

Buyer psychology: it's about "motive, cause and intent". If your motive is to make the
sale, or the pressure comes from your boss, the buyer's "brain will perceive me as a
threat". "What am I doing that is causing my buyer to perceive me as a threat?" Buyers
"don't want to be sold, coerced, managed, pressured, convinced, closed". They want to
"make the right decision well".

Selling: "Do not sell. Facilitate buying." "My job is never to convince. My job is to
create the conditions so you convince yourself." "The job of selling is service, not
servitude."

Ethics: "If the truth won't sell it, don't sell it." He is against using a buyer's pain
to get a sale: "Would that be an act of love or coercion?" Bringing a deal forward to
hit your number makes the customer "pay the negative price for your failure to prospect
months before". "Sales is a force for good."

Customers for life: "Do not be transactional." "Prospect for a lifetime customer who's
going to be your customer 5, 8, 10 years down the road." Customers who "repeat, they
expand and they refer" and "would hate to be without" you.

Leadership and metrics: be "really really ruthlessly hard on the process", and don't
blame salespeople for doing what you told them to do. Dials, demos and revenue are
"supply side metrics" the customer "gives one jot about". CRM "is a thinly disguised
vain attempt to give management the illusion of control". "You are a leader if you
empower other people to make good decisions well."

Systems: "Go to market is an organism. It is not a bunch of silos." Keep a failure log:
write your failure down and that's fine; "if you hide it, you get fired."

Buyer safety: "create the conditions where they can feel safe". "If they don't feel safe
you'll just get kicked into the long grass." Perceived risk: "vulnerability times
uncertainty equals perceived risk". He uses Charlie Green's trust equation: "credibility
plus reliability plus intimacy over self orientation". If your focus is your quota,
trust goes down.

Do nothing is a competitor: buyers compare options, "one of which may be do nothing".
Understand "the competitive landscape in which they trade, which includes do nothing".

Statement of work, not a proposal: "The demo, like the proposal, is not a sales tool.
Both of those are confirmations of the order." Co-create a "build your own statement of
work" with "their voice, their vision, their fingerprints all over it". "My job is to be
a scribe. It's not to be an author of fiction or hope." "People never argue with their
own data."

Quota: "your customer does not give a damn about your quota, your investors, your
enterprise value, your exit timetable." "Our job is not to make quota. Our job is to
identify the right type of future customer and create the conditions so that they buy
eventually, if it's right for them."

Buying committees: citing a procurement chief, Mike Lander, there is "the evaluation
committee", "the decision making committee" and "the allocation committee", and you need
relationships with all three.

Where to focus: existing customers first, then "winning back past customers because
you're probably already on their system. You don't have to jump through procurement and
finance and IT." New customers last.

Price's law (not his, but he teaches it): "the square root of the number of people in
your organization will generate 50 percent plus of your output. So if you have 10
salespeople, 3 will produce 50 percent." Lose a top performer and there's "a 50 percent
probability you're going to lose another one within 6 months".

Time with customers: his baseline is salespeople "spending 60 percent of their time in
front of the customer". In most businesses, "at the high end it'd be around 21 percent".

Ramp time: citing Phil McGowan's PhD, "it takes 3.2 years for a salesperson to hit full
stride".

Partners: "one partner in my book is worth 100 customers if you sell well."

AI: used badly, "you fill the pipeline with more dross" and "amplify the noise, not the
signal". "Don't just take what it says. Go to 3 or 4 different LLMs."

Forecasts built on optimism: "Bad news travels slowly. Optimism travels quickly. Maybe
becomes likely. Likely becomes committed. Committed becomes the forecast." "The seller
says the deal is committed. The manager accepts the confidence. The forecast absorbs the
number." "A forecast can tell you what you expect to happen. It doesn't necessarily tell
you why performance is disappointing or which intervention will improve it."

Buyer evidence, not seller confidence: a deal where "the buyer has missed three agreed
dates. The decision-maker hasn't been involved. Nobody can say what needs to happen for
the customer to proceed. But the opportunity is still in the forecast."

Revenue quality: "Not all revenue is good revenue. Booked revenue is not the same as
secure revenue." "Revenue quality slips before revenue does." Interest is not
commitment, a contract is not activation, and closed revenue is not good revenue.

Founder bottleneck: "the founder becomes the bottleneck without ever meaning to". "The
team waits. Managers second-guess. Decisions slow down." The goal is a revenue system
that scales "without every deal, exception and decision running through 1 or 2 people",
replacing "founder dependence with founder design".

Activity is not selling: "We can measure the hell out of selling": calls, emails,
meetings, demos and stage changes. That tells you what salespeople did, not whether the
buyer moved.
