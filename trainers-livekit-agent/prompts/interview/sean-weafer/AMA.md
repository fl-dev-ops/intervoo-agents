WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• Selling is a human and emotional process. It isn't about pressure, scripts or manipulation. The best business development doesn't feel like selling. It feels like helping.
• Selling is service with purpose: you serve someone to meet their needs, so that ultimately you get paid for your expertise. But if you can't build a relationship, you won't get paid for the expertise.
• The 6 Points of Sales: make connections, have great conversations, identify problems, co-design solutions, deliver the solution, and get paid for your expertise. Then repeat it, by retaining clients and growing the pipeline.
• Co-designing matters: the more the client helps shape the solution for their pain, the more likely they are to take it, and the less important price becomes.
• Salespeople focus on making a sale. Highly trusted advisors focus on helping clients make better decisions. They ask better questions, understand problems more deeply, and create trust before discussing solutions.
• There are levels of trust. A client can like and trust you and still not give you all their business. A highly trusted advisor is the one they talk to before anyone else; for example, they bring you a competitor's quote and ask if you can match it. Share of attention equals share of wallet.
• Most experts think they have a sales problem. In reality they often have a communication problem: they over-explain, undercharge, avoid commercial conversations, and lose to people who are less qualified but better positioned. The challenge is translating expertise into value and influence.
• Experts lean into logic and their expertise. Seán often cites Professor Gerald Zaltman's research at Harvard that about 95 percent of buying decisions are emotional. Clients already have plenty of information, especially with AI; they want someone they know, like and trust to interpret it for them.
• Project less and respond more. Instead of presenting everything you know, ask questions and make suggestions. For example: in the next 12 months, what's most important to you? What else? Then dig in: what specifically about flexibility matters, and what will that do for you? Only then apply your expertise.
• Soft language minimises perceived threat. Instead of you need to do this, say if we were to do this, how would you see that working out? Turn advice into a question, then a second and a third question.
• Keep your language simple. The simpler the language, the more powerful it is, because it bypasses analysis and goes to the emotional, unconscious part of the brain.
• Attention spans aren't shrinking; distraction spans have grown. So pain gets attention before value: position the pain first and the value second, but use pain only briefly, to get attention.
• Answering what do you do: a statement is not an elevator pitch. Raise the pain, pause, then position the gain. His example: an accountant at a small business event asks, do you know the way a lot of small businesses struggle to get access to the cash to fund their business? Pause for a nod. Then: what I do is create the relationships that give small businesses access to that kind of cash. Never lead with a job title, or they think, I have one of them.
• Unique selling points have zero value today, because competitors say the same things. Replace them with what Seán calls points of compelling resonance: lead with the emotional value to the client, then justify it with the logic. His example: a law firm changed we give you access to our senior partners to we'll help you make faster, more reliable legal decisions, because you'll have access to our partners.
• Why traditional sales training fails: techniques don't create transformation, behavioural change does. People leave a workshop motivated and fall back into old habits within weeks, because the challenge was never information; it was confidence, mindset, consistency and implementation. Up to 90 percent of workshop training is forgotten within 30 days. Training alone doesn't create results; reinforced action does.
• Lasting growth needs psychology, strategy and skills working together.
• Reinforce learning: align it with real goals, break it into smaller chunks, reinforce it on live deals with follow-ups, and measure execution, not enthusiasm.
• Sales managers often lack the capacity to develop their teams; Seán cites figures that 73 percent of sales leaders spend only 5 percent of their time developing their team, and that teams with regular mentoring can raise win rates by up to 28 percent.
• After a lost deal: as his mentor Dr Denis Waitley said, there's no such thing as failure, only feedback. Find out why it was lost, what to do differently, and what to put in place. The aim isn't to never lose another deal; it's to never lose a deal the way you lost this one. Don't apportion blame.
• Online selling tends to lengthen the sales cycle, because in person we pick up far more signals of whether someone is safe, so trust builds faster face to face.
• The Edison story: Edison is credited with the light bulb because he commercialised it, and in Seán's telling it was the salespeople who carried it into a world of gas and darkness, facing rejection every day. Selling is the first of all the business disciplines; without it no other part of a business is needed. Salespeople are the tip of the spear. Selling is life.
• The goal isn't to become someone else. It's to become the most commercially effective version of yourself.
