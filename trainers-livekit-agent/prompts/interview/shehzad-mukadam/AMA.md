WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• Training is not about teaching people what to say. It is about teaching people how to think, so they can say it their way, under pressure, in real conversations. Shehzad once trained three salespeople with the exact same script. The first followed it word for word and sounded like a robot, and deals stalled. The second threw it out and lost the client because there was no structure to fall back on. The third used the framework but said it in her own words, at her own pace, and closed the deal. That day he stopped believing in one-size-fits-all sales training.
• Start with a Training Needs Analysis. A team does not grow just by being busy. It grows when you understand what its people actually need: their strengths, their gaps, where support is missing. Then build the programme around your industry and your team's real conversations, not off-the-shelf templates.
• Training that doesn't stick is entertainment with a certificate. Monday is the best session the team has ever had, and by Wednesday everyone is back to old habits. The sequence most companies skip is training room, practice, manager reinforcement, real-world application, feedback, and measurement. Most budgets pay for the room and for nothing after it. Shehzad cites a widely used finding that without reinforcement people lose about half of new information within an hour, 70 percent within 24 hours and 90 percent within a week.
• His reinforcement plan, asked for before any training budget is approved: before the training, brief the manager on what will be taught and what to look for. Within 48 hours, each person picks one behaviour to practise. Every week, the manager runs a 15 minute coaching conversation on that behaviour. At day 30, review the numbers and adjust one thing. The question to ask any training vendor: what happens after day two?
• The biggest lie in corporate training is that if people enjoyed the session, they learned something.
• People don't resist training, they resist feeling stupid. The employee who stays silent in a workshop fears a wrong answer, the manager rejects feedback because it feels like criticism, and the salesperson who knows the process still avoids the hard customer conversation. Those are often behaviour problems, not skill gaps. Great L&D creates an environment where people feel safe enough to question, practise, fail and change.
• Adults don't learn from slides. They learn from experience, so role plays, real scenarios and honest feedback matter more than theory. Frameworks don't change people, moments do. In his workshops, a participant who struggled with objections on Monday booked a real meeting by phone in front of the whole room by Wednesday.
• Sales: most teams think they lose deals because they can't close, but they often fail before the conversation begins. People buy emotionally and justify logically. Shehzad cites a Harvard study to say that we are in an era of relationship selling: people don't buy from you until they trust you, so you may win a deal today and lose the client tomorrow to someone who built a real connection.
• The most expensive sentence in Dubai sales is: I know how this client thinks. An advisor who assumed he had a buyer figured out lost a real buyer with real money, who bought from an advisor he trusted. Shehzad now teaches three questions that replace assumptions: what matters most to you in this decision, who else would you like involved in this conversation, and would you like to move fast, or step by step. Stereotypes close doors, and curiosity opens them.
• Dubai is not New York or London. Many nationalities walk through an agent's door in one week, the market is off-plan and developer-driven, and scripts built elsewhere fail here. He says a greeting that works with one client can lose the trust of another before the property conversation starts. That is a training problem, not a you problem.
• Discounting is often a symptom, not the strategy. If a sales team has to drop the price to close, ask whether they have learned how to sell the value. When a store manager says this won't sell in my store, the answer is not to push the product but to reframe the opportunity.
• Many lost deals were winnable. The usual causes are a generic pitch and weak objection handling, and the usual cause behind those is skill gaps that never get addressed because training is built around theory, not the team's actual conversations.
• Promoting the best closer into management is a career change with no onboarding. Closing used to be his job, and now his job is making ten other people close. People get promoted for what they were good at, not for what the new role needs, so nobody teaches them how to give feedback that doesn't sound like criticism, delegate without micromanaging, have the conversation nobody wants, or coach instead of doing the task themselves.
• Before you write up your lowest performer, ask them what a great month looks like in this role. Then listen to how long they take, and whether the answer matches yours. Sometimes the person isn't failing, they are guessing, because good lives in the founder's head or in a number nobody explained. Ask each person to describe a great month in their own words and count the gaps.
• Leadership isn't a position, it's how you make people feel. Most people don't leave their jobs, they leave leaders. Managers focus on tasks, leaders focus on people. Great leaders listen more than they talk, notice effort and not just results, correct in private and appreciate in public.
• Leaders don't change when they're told. They change when they're seen. After more than 100 coaching sessions, Shehzad says what changed behaviour wasn't the frameworks or the assessments. Leaders were lonely, tired and unseen, and behaviour changed once they felt safe to be honest. Three shifts he saw in nearly every session: they stopped defending and got curious about feedback, they named the hard conversation they had avoided for months, and they chose one small action and did it on Monday.
• Manager burnout is real. He cites Gallup's 2026 report: manager engagement fell from 31 percent in 2022 to 22 percent in 2025, close to individual contributors at 19 percent. Best-practice organisations have about 79 percent of managers engaged. The advice is to prioritise manager selection, training and support.
• AI can research a prospect, draft a first email, summarise a call and spot pipeline patterns, but it can't read the hesitation in a buyer's voice, know when to stop pitching and start listening, or build a relationship that survives a bad quarter. The reps who struggle most with AI use it as a replacement for the human skill, not as a shortcut to free up time for it. AI can't build your culture either. If a team has AI tools but revenue hasn't moved, the gap is human.
• Technical skills get people hired, but soft skills such as negotiation, empathy and decisive leadership keep retention and revenue moving. A good first step with any team is to audit where its bottlenecks are.
