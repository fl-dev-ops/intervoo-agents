WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• The 80 20 flip. In traditional software, a team spends about 80 percent of its time building and 20 percent on what happens after, such as fixing bugs. In AI it flips: maybe 20 percent building, especially with AI assistants, and 80 percent on what she calls calibration, which means learning how users really behave with the product, how well it is doing, and feeding that back as a flywheel. She says this is what separates a good AI product from a prototype or a good demo.
• Look at your data. If there is one takeaway for anybody building AI products, it is to take the time to look at your data. People test a few cases, see that it seems to work, and don't look at the data distribution they get from real users. She says you can't just ask an LLM to find user patterns, build evals for itself and update itself; it doesn't work that way. Someone has to understand the workflows and the context by hand, and she calls that manual set-up work super underrated. To a software engineer who has never done AI, hearing look at your data is a huge revelation.
• Two differences from traditional software. First, non-determinism: both the input and the output are far less predictable. Users can talk to an AI product in endless ways instead of clicking buttons, and the model can answer in many ways, so there is a much larger surface area for errors. Second, the agency control trade-off: the more decision-making you hand to an AI system, the more control you give up as a human, so the system has to earn that trust before you hand more over.
• Continuous calibration, continuous development, which she shortens to CC and CD. On the development side, scope the capability and curate a small dataset of expected inputs and outputs, which also shows whether your team agrees on how the product should behave; then build the application, design evaluation metrics and deploy. On the calibration side, look at the behaviour you did not expect, spot error patterns, fix them, and design new metrics only for patterns that keep emerging. Some errors, such as a tool that was defined badly, you can simply fix and move on. Start with low agency and high control and give the system more agency only as it earns trust.
• Build step by step. She recommends starting small, getting an iteration going and building a flywheel. Starting small forces you to think about the problem you are solving; in all the advancements, one easy slippery slope is to keep thinking about the complexity of the solution and forget the problem. She also says: if it can be a workflow, don't make it autonomous; if a simple model call can solve it and you can audit the decisions, a simple call is enough.
• Human in the loop and the flywheel. Decide which use cases go through a human and which the AI can handle on its own, and log what the human does, so you can improve the system without hurting the user experience or eroding trust. It is not about being the first company in your market to have an agent; it is about having the right flywheels in place to improve over time.
• Evals. She describes evals as checks that tell you whether a system is doing what it was designed to do. It feels like testing in traditional software, and that intuition is partly right, but testing AI products works differently because of non-determinism. She distinguishes model evals from product evals, and says evaluation is a process while evaluation metrics are the dimensions you focus on in that process. She calls evals the most important skill for AI engineers and one of the most misunderstood.
• Harness engineering. In tools like Claude Code, Codex or ChatGPT, the scaffolding around the model is called a harness and is written in ordinary software code. The model is the part that decides what should happen, and the harness is the part that makes it happen. Her car picture: the harness is like a good dashboard and steering so you can see your speed, plus seat belts, crumple zones, mirrors and lights, so the vehicle is not only functional but comes with a whole operating system.
• The shifts in AI engineering as she tells them: prompt engineering, then context engineering, which deals with data, memory and context rot, then harness engineering, then loop engineering and graph engineering. Loop engineering is where you stop being the one who starts everything and checking what came back, and let the agent run in a loop. She says harness and loop are not fundamental technology changes but ways of optimising how you work with the system.
• Context matters. She says you must understand context and workflows, and that taking the time to do some set-up by hand so agents can perform at their best is underrated.
• Is learning to code still worth it? Yes. She calls it the one level up rule: each time a new tool has threatened coders, from COBOL in 1959 to fourth generation languages to no-code tools, the skill moved one level up to the people who adapted and demand for software grew. When building gets cheaper, more people want more software. She says what matters now is the ability to think clearly about your problem and direct the tools that build it.
• Traditional developers. She says traditional developers have strong design thinking: they treat the model as an API and build everything around it, including security, design and scale. Much of AI engineering is traditional software engineering with caveats, such as looking at your data and building evals that look different, and she says traditional engineers get it bang on if they treat the model as a non-deterministic API.
• Foundations before hype. She is annoyed by people who hang on to one idea, such as RAG is all you need, or agents are all you need, or evals are all you need, without understanding the depth. Someone who knows agents but not what RAG is should brush up on foundations. She warns against the claim that RAG is dead.
• Becoming an AI engineer. In the AI engineering interview she describes, the edge is judgment and really thinking before building, because building has become easy. Companies have moved away from typical LeetCode style questions, because AI can do them, and multi-agent systems are not like microservices.
• Leaders. She told a story of a CEO who blocked every morning from 4 to 6 AM for catching up with AI, and says leaders have to get back to being hands on. You must be comfortable that your intuitions might be wrong, and that you are probably the dumbest person in the room.
• Implementation is going to be very cheap in the next few years, so nail down your design, your judgment and your taste.
• What to automate first, her scoring method. She says AI is super useful only in places that are high volume, low variability and low stakes, and that you must choose which systems you automate. To pick, list the repeated workflows you run and score each from one to five on three questions: how often it happens, what it costs if it goes wrong, and how often the steps change. Anything that scores well on all three is good for automation, and the rest you leave alone. Make your first build the most boring, high volume task on the list. Work that is specific to your voice, like how you write to an upset customer or frame hard feedback, is different: either codify your style with real examples and phrasing, or do it by hand, and never automate everything and hope it sounds like you.
• Your first automation won't work perfectly, and that is normal. Her example: a support ticket automation is right about eighty percent of the time and keeps misreading refund requests as general questions, so refunds sit unanswered for days and customers lose trust. The team's first instinct is to scrap it and start over, but the fix is a few weeks of small corrections and calibration, about twenty minutes of work a week. Automation still needs human oversight.
• Increase agency over time, from high control to higher agency. Her example is a marketing assistant. Version one drafts email, ad or social copy from prompts and a human reviews and publishes. Version two builds and runs a multi-step campaign. Version three launches A B tests and auto-optimises across channels, with humans guiding and giving feedback throughout. Coding assistants evolved the same way, from inline completions, to larger blocks for human review, to applying changes and opening pull requests, and she says that agency was earned over time.
• Human in the loop by risk. Classify an agent's actions by risk: low risk actions such as summarising customer conversations or retrieving an authorised proposal can happen autonomously, while sensitive or irreversible actions such as sending a contract to a customer need explicit approval before they run. The agent pauses before a sensitive tool call. A good first agent is one where even if it routes something wrong, humans can take control and undo it.
• AI security. She says AI security risks are no longer theoretical. Her framework starts with least privilege: restrict the agent's own permissions to what the task needs, a sales agent needs customer and sales data but not employee salaries, and also enforce the permissions of the actual person using it, so the head of sales can get company-wide forecasts and a junior salesperson asking the same question is blocked. A shared service account is a trap, because the system then enforces that account's permissions and not the employee's. She also covers context minimisation, data egress controls, prompt injection and keeping trusted instructions separate from untrusted information, structured extraction, tool allowlists, secure tool wrappers against excessive agency, human approvals for sensitive actions, and security evaluations and red teaming. Excessive agency means the agent has more capability or autonomy than it needs.
• Is RAG dead? She says the internet cannot agree: half say it is dead and half say it is one of the most important things to learn. RAG, retrieval augmented generation, is the open book version of AI: before it answers, it looks up your knowledge base, because on its own the model only knows what it was trained on and knows nothing of your company's documents. She says bigger context windows don't kill retrieval, naive RAG is fading while agentic retrieval gets stronger, retrieval doesn't always mean a vector database, and the best teams are evolving RAG into a knowledge runtime with sourcing, confidence, contradiction handling and access control. She also names where RAG systems actually fail.
• Fine-tuning. She says it is either dead or about to become one of the most important skills, depending on who you ask, and that it matters which approach and use case people mean. Fine-tuning is not new. One argument says RAG and long context windows make it unnecessary, since general models can classify, summarise and extract from instructions and a few examples, and RAG gives them your company's information. She also covers supervised fine-tuning, preference optimisation, reinforcement learning and LoRA, how fine-tuning differs from RAG and context engineering, why companies invest in smaller specialised models, and why better foundation models could make fine-tuning obsolete.
• AI system design, her five layers. She has interviewed hundreds of people for AI roles, first at AWS and now at her company, and the candidates who don't get hired know what RAG, MCP or tool calling are but struggle to apply them to a real business problem or explain how to scale a system to production. Her framework, shown on a customer support agent: layer one is scope, assumptions and agent autonomy; layer two is architecture, which she calls Know, Act, Remember and Coordinate, covering retrieval, tools with permissions and trust boundaries, memory and how they connect; layer three is observability, logging and evals; layer four is production scaling, reliability and security; layer five is optimising latency, cost and quality. Before anyone throws around terms like RAG, agents or multi-agent systems, scope the problem first. Don't memorise one architecture: take the questions from each layer and apply them to a different use case.
• Memory. Models cannot remember your earlier conversations; they only know what is in the current context window, so you design memory outside the model. One simple way is persistent files, such as a user profile of stable preferences, and you need authentication and a persistent user ID so every conversation and memory belongs to the right person.
• Cutting token costs, her cheat sheet. She says you can slash token costs a great deal at the application layer without hosting your own models, by fixing the agent harness. First measure: track cost per successful task, not only cost per million tokens, and build an eval set so you know optimisations don't hurt quality. Then reuse and reduce context: use prompt caching with stable prefixes, load tools on demand, and filter tool responses before adding them to context. Then build custom compaction, turning long histories into structured working states of facts, decisions and next actions. Then spend selectively: route simple tasks to smaller models, limit output length and move non-urgent work to asynchronous workflows. Before spending weeks on fine-tuning or your own infrastructure, look at how your harness manages context.
• Evals, the two camps she sees. One camp makes evals extremely pedantic, with a process, rubric, taxonomy and ten layers of discussion for everything, which falls apart once real users do weird things. The other treats evals like a checklist: a few LLM judges, a dashboard and some scores, which doesn't tell you what to fix and can become a cost bomb. Her aim is an improvement flywheel and not another checklist. She also describes three ways to build evals: human review, code-based checks and LLM as a judge, and says evals are the most important skill for AI engineers.
• Getting more from Claude Code and similar agents, her five levels. First audit your context so the agent stops answering confidently from stale or conflicting data, and write a short identity file yourself instead of letting the agent bloat it. Second, optimise prompts and use prompt caching. Third, isolate tasks in separate conversations so irrelevant context does not pile up. Fourth, recover when a conversation goes off track using rewinds, forks and handoffs. Fifth, loop engineering, where agents repeat work and check results with clear goals, memory and stopping conditions. She also says to check the agent is even loading a skill, test in a clean environment, and run tasks with and without the skill.
• A personal AI agent, her parts. An identity file that tells the agent who you are, tools connected safely with limited permissions such as narrow Gmail scopes, and skills and triggers that make it sharper. Running a company on Codex, she describes a knowledge base or wiki for the agent, connecting it to email, calendar and other tools, and turning repeated prompts into reusable skills and automations.
• Local AI and hybrid systems. The shift is toward hybrid: companies run their own or local models for work that is private, repetitive or high volume, where cost and control matter, and call frontier models for work that has to be right. One large model can orchestrate while smaller local models run the sub-agents.
• MCP, the Model Context Protocol, is an open standard that gives AI applications a consistent way to connect with external tools and data, and she says it is growing fast in 2026.
• Forward deployed engineering. She says it is probably the most overhyped AI job. To ground it she pulled more than a thousand live job listings, and she busts four myths around the role. She says she works with a forward deployed mindset: entering a company, understanding its ecosystem and building solutions with it.
• What companies hire for in AI roles. Her builder's loop in six steps: frame the right problem, starting with the business problem, the user, the constraints and the outcome, not a model or a tool; design the system intentionally, choosing architecture, context, autonomy and human involvement; define what good looks like before building; build an improvement flywheel with evals, production signals and error analysis; design AI augmented workflows, deciding where AI supports thinking and where human judgment stays in control; and build repeatable operating systems from effective workflows. Play with the tools, but don't get so obsessed that you lose sight of the bigger picture.
• Advice to aspiring AI engineers. Please stop filling GitHub with AI generated projects with little engineering thought. She doesn't care how much code AI wrote, but you must understand it and explain your choices. Build projects that solve problems companies actually have, ideally relevant to the company you are applying to. Show your engineering decisions in a short document with the architecture, tradeoffs, what didn't work and what you would change. Make the project ridiculously easy to run, because interviewers will actually clone it. And show that you tested it, with edge cases and known limitations. Projects that stand out on a resume include a document intelligence pipeline, enterprise search with RAG and conversation intelligence with voice AI.
• Think before building. When everybody is busy building, be the person who thinks before building. Don't build AI prototypes just for the heck of it; build thoughtfully, starting with the problem. A problem-first mindset is your moat. Enterprises don't want impressive demos, they want reliable, predictable outcomes.
• Auditing how work gets done. She says much of AI engineering comes down to auditing how work gets done and then helping people learn and adapt as the processes change, so companies should also educate their teams on how work is changing.
