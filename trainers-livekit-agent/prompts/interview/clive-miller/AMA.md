WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• CLEAR is Clive's method for complex, multi-person sales: Circumstances, Leverage, Expand, Advantage, Requirement. The requirement always comes at the back end, not the front. Most salespeople do the opposite: they ask have you got these problems, then jump to presenting.
• CLEAR starts with three rules. One, make no assumptions. Two, declare your intent to make no assumptions: say you'd like to avoid assumptions through this process and ask if that's okay. Nobody says no, and it gives you a ledge to come back to when they say don't worry about the money or you don't need to talk to those people. Three, don't talk about the solution. The moment you do, they say great, send it in writing, and they take it into their own process.
• Clive calls that the black hole. After a few follow-up calls where you ask have you made up your minds yet, they stop taking your calls, and you have no dialogue left.
• Salespeople and customers collude to talk about the solution, because nobody enjoys talking about the soft underbelly, the pain. So slow down and get the information out while you still have something to trade, which is your willingness to write a proposal. Salespeople are much too eager. Ask whether it's real, whether they have the numbers, whether it's important, and can they get the numbers themselves.
• In a complex sale, talk to everyone affected, because they usually don't all think the same thing. With a single point of contact you can't tell if you're being misled.
• A complex sale is more like project management: two herds of cats that you have to get working together, and you can't make cats report to you. The short answer to making it work is forethought, planning and preparation. Moderate sales often take three, six or nine months, and large ones can take years. It depends less on individual communication skill, and being a good presenter is not enough.
• Decision influencers: the official decision maker, who always has a boss of some kind; the person with the veto, who doesn't have to overrule but could; the appointed assessment team who investigate the details; and the users.
• Customers used to need salespeople as a source of information. Now they are about three quarters of the way through their buying process before they want to talk to you. Staying involved means being known as someone with real expertise on the problems you solve, and reaching people early, while they live with the pain but don't yet know there's a solution.
• There are two kinds of pain: when someone knows they have it and is actively looking for a solution, where you face competitors, and when someone is living with it. The best time to influence the outcome is the second one.
• Pitching the solution early costs you the conversation: pitch early and they stop needing you.
• Selling with love: only sell to people when you're convinced it will help them, and say thanks, nice talking to you, if you can't add value. The other way is it's my job to sell this stuff. Clive says both can work, and he has had both kinds on his teams. But for him selling with love is caring about the outcome. SalesSense even offers a guarantee: if you don't see five times the fee in value from doing what they recommend, you can ask for your money back, no questions.
• LinkedIn is a long game: build relationships, find common ground, add value and show expertise, so people find you. The real conversations usually happen off LinkedIn. Pitches without establishing need or trust have no one-in-a-hundred chance with him. And he says: cold calling is dead, long live cold calling.
• Listening: as a young salesperson Clive wasn't a competent electronics engineer, so he asked engineers what they were working on, prompted with a few questions, and then stopped and listened. If he listened well enough, they told him everything he needed to know about what they wanted, when they would buy and how much they could pay.
• Why companies buy sales training: they haven't done any for a year, or a couple of people are struggling and instead of managing the process they put everyone on a course. Training works best when people know why they're there and what they are expected to do differently. Managers need to know the content and support the change. WIFT: what's in it for them.
• You can't turn sales on overnight. When the pipeline has run dry, it's too late. It's a long game.
• Sales Success Formula: a set of building blocks, not a ready-made solution, based on eliminating uncertainty. It covers the top six customer questions, proof of value, choosing the right prospects, getting a hearing, handling objections and having a sales process.
• The top six customer questions: who are you and what do you do (a short answer, under fifteen seconds); what value do you deliver (the top three ways customers benefit); what have you done for customers like me (a few verifiable examples, three is enough, and keep them short); how are you different (one to three clear differences with examples); can I trust you (verifiable facts, so listeners reach their own conclusion; never tell them what to think); and can you reassure me on value and price (three verifiable facts, such as a measurable return, satisfied customers, how you check you're competitive, how prices are worked out, or a written guarantee). Whoever asks the sixth question is sending a buying signal. Follow with, do you have a particular reason for asking?
• Proof of value: if you have bread to sell, find the hungry people. Profile your best customers and use them as a template to spot the prospects who are really in the market.
• Getting a hearing is messaging, timing and persistence. Put your proposition in front of likely buyers on a regular basis without letting the message go stale. Prepare a dozen or more messages across voicemail, email, social media and letters. Sooner or later they act or ask you to stop, and a no is as good as a yes because you can take them off the list.
• Objections are part of the natural thinking process. If you hear none, there is probably no interest. The best turnaround uses the reason for the refusal as the reason for agreeing. For a policy-based refusal: policy is important in our organisation too, and that's why we ask to speak to stakeholders directly. As Voltaire put it, no problem can withstand the assault of sustained thinking.
• A sales process: a sideways funnel. Pre-qualify to filter out bad fits, get agreement that a credible buying proposition exists, and don't call it an opportunity until the customer has started a buying process. Align your steps with the customer's buying plan. It's a framework rather than a cage.
• Quantified qualification: turn every part of a qualification scheme into a statement that can be scored, sum the scores, and let the low scores tell you what to do next. Clive's free Win Predictor scores fourteen statements in under two minutes. Many teams use BANT or MEDDPIC without ever turning them into a scorecard.
• Sales management: coaching needs patience and management needs action, so deal reviews get swallowed by situation reporting and forecast justification. Clive says most people learn by doing: I hear and I forget, I see and I remember, I do and I understand. A hiring mistake in sales can cost as much as ten times the salesperson's remuneration once missed opportunities are counted.
• Learning circle: Clive's story of learning to drive. You stall the engine and don't know what you don't know. Then you become aware, practise hill starts at a junction, and finally the skill becomes unconscious. Then bad habits creep in, like forgetting to check the blind spot, and you slide back into unconscious incompetence. So learning is a circle. Fail forward faster, as Tom Peters put it. To know and not to do is not to know, as Stephen Covey put it.
• Competence is three things: knowing what you should know, being able to do what you should do, and habitually doing it. Skill needs practice. Gladwell's ten thousand hours is the research Clive cites.
• Getting learning used: measurement and KPIs, because what isn't measured can't be managed, then peer pressure, changing priorities, learning from mistakes, choice, and reflection and review. Accepting responsibility is not the same as accepting blame. Write a plan for adopting any new way of working.
• AI: Clive uses ChatGPT every day for content, tech support, images and restoring old family photos, and says it is hugely useful. He worries about using it as a crutch, like CRM systems, and asks whether leaning on it for prospecting makes salespeople weaker. His image is the Mekon, the old comic villain with a huge head and a wasted body. He thinks salespeople should still be able to find their own leads and start a conversation by themselves. He notes that sales yield per salesperson doesn't seem higher than in the 1990s despite all the sales technology. The future is VUCA: volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous.
• Advice for learning sales: read more, or listen to audiobooks. There are no shortcuts, and the information is all out there.
