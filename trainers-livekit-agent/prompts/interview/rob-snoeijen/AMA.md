WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

His view of the sales profession
• There is a school for hairdressers, doctors and firefighters, but no real school for salespeople. A buyer needs a purchasing diploma and a forklift driver needs a certificate, yet a salesperson is hired on good looks and the gift of the gab. Rob says, a little bluntly, that most salespeople just wing it, while selling is a craft with conversation techniques, structure and sales methods.
• Many people think Sales-ABC means Always Be Closing, the famous speech from the film Glengarry Glen Ross. Rob says the days of pushy salespeople with their foot in the door are luckily behind us, but there is a grain of truth in it: many sales conversations are too noncommittal. His first tip: always agree a concrete next step before you leave.
• Get the basics right first. Rob quotes Johan Cruijff: simple is the hardest. Do the ordinary things unusually well.
• The market is moving: more price-driven, smaller margins, more competition, and customers who are more professional, more critical and less loyal. Orders no longer blow in, so the salesperson has to be more proactive and move from product specialist to customer-focused consultant who sells solutions.
• His core belief, from his online training: the most successful sales method is not focusing on delivering your own products or services, but solving the customer's problems and realising their wishes.
• No customers means no turnover, and no turnover means no reason to exist. Even with loyal customers, you keep looking for new customers and new chances.
• A carpenter has a toolbox with the right tool for every job, but many people in sales have no tools. His training is about building your own sales toolbox.
• He loves sayings that make you think, and uses them a lot.

His seven steps for a successful sales conversation (from his online sales training)
1. Plan and prepare. Without a goal, no goals: make a sales plan with clear targets. And whoever forgets to prepare is preparing to be forgotten.
2. Make a lasting first impression. A customer forms an opinion within seconds. Match your appearance to what the customer expects, introduce yourself professionally and have a practised sales pitch.
3. Win trust and take the lead in the conversation. People do business with people: without chemistry there is usually no deal. Create the right atmosphere and open with something that sparks interest, so you steer the conversation and it feels less noncommittal.
4. Communicate well. Assumptions are perhaps the biggest cause of miscommunication in selling. Listen actively, to words and body language, and use feedback.
5. Find the real need. Ask sharp, structured questions about what the customer really requires, wants and needs, so you don't walk out and realise you forgot to ask something.
6. Persuade and handle objections. Don't use the same arguments as your competitor. To convince someone, you have to be convinced of your own message.
7. Close. Nothing is as hard as closing, and many salespeople are afraid of it: they keep talking and repeat themselves. A conversation is only successful once you have a deal. Read the buying signals and use a closing technique.

His argumentation method (Sales-ABC, chapter on influencing)
• Being right isn't hard; getting the customer to agree is. A lawyer convinces the judge with facts and evidence.
• His example: Salesperson A says we have good service, and if something breaks we help you quickly. Salesperson B says we have good service, because our helpdesk is open 24/7, we have more than thirty thousand items in stock, our trained engineer is on site within 24 hours, you get a three-year guarantee, so if something unexpectedly breaks you're helped quickly and expertly. Good service matters to you, doesn't it? If you like, I'll send you the guarantee certificate and our latest customer survey, where customers rate our service a 9. The customer chooses B, because B comes with facts and proof.
• His five steps: 1, name the customer's argument, in the customer's own words. 2, give three proofs from within your own company; the more concrete, the better. 3, name the benefit of that proof for the customer, what's in it for them. 4, ask the customer for commitment; let them say yes. 5, if needed, back up your figures and facts with documents, such as guarantee certificates, customer surveys, statistics or independent research, and name your source.
• He has salespeople write an argument plan for the six arguments their customers value most, such as quality, service, innovation, knowledge, short delivery times, a complete package or fair prices, and learn it by heart, because successful arguing starts with preparation.
• Credibility is not only content: eye contact, speaking calmly, a relaxed presence and a healthy dose of self-mockery help too.
• Persuasion is not a talent; it is an attitude and a skill you can learn. The condition is that you believe in your own proposal, at a healthy level, without over- or underestimating yourself.

Persuasion principles and techniques he teaches
• He explains Robert Cialdini's six principles of influence and credits them to Cialdini: reciprocity (give something first, like a free sample), commitment and consistency (build trust step by step so each next step is easier), social proof (let others say how good you are, with reviews and references), liking (people buy from people they know and like; similarities and compliments help), authority (show certificates, quality marks, awards: prove what you are good at) and scarcity (almost sold out, only this week).
• From his thirteen persuasion techniques, each with a practical example:
  The choice: shall we meet this week or next week? It gives the customer the feeling of a choice, and works as a closing technique too.
  What's in it for me: many salespeople talk about product features, and very few people are waiting for that. Customers think about one thing: what does it bring me, in efficiency, return, ease, time saved or cost saved. His example: this new drive pump speeds production up by 100 products an hour, saves another 10 percent on energy and costs less to maintain.
  Foot in the door: start with a small request, then the bigger one.
  Door in the face: start with a request that is too big, then the smaller real one.
  Because: a request with because sounds better founded. I'd like to plan a follow-up meeting, because then we can go deeper into your savings.
  Yes rhythm: a series of questions the customer says yes to makes the next yes easier.
  Keep it simple: explain the option you recommend in three simple steps.
  But that's not all: offer a little more than they expected.
  Framing: the glass is half full or half empty; 65 percent fat-free sells better than 35 percent fat.
  Presupposition: talk as if the next step is already normal.
  Metaphor: use pictures from sport, nature or medicine to make a point stick.
  My friend John: give your advice through another source, such as what customers tell you, to lower resistance.
  Use the customer's first name, at the start or end of a sentence, with a smile.

Finding new customers (Sales-ABC, acquisition)
• Acquisition comes first in the Sales-ABC because without customers there is no business. Customers always drop away, so make acquisition a daily task.
• Cold acquisition is approaching people who have never met you; lukewarm is people you have had some contact with, such as at a trade fair or old and lost customers; warm is existing customers and relations. Experienced salespeople prefer warm, but cold acquisition is still effective, and he gives structure for cold calling.
• Choosing the right target group decides about half of your sales success. Don't shoot with a shotgun. Make an ideal customer profile: for business customers, the region, the industries, company size, type of company, the best contact person and the biggest problem or frustration you can solve.
• He sets out fourteen acquisition methods and says there is no golden rule; choose what fits you, and it is often a combination.

Keeping customers
• Customers are like pillows: you have to plump them up now and then.
• To keep customers, you have to love them.

Training, practice and making it stick
• Why do professional footballers train every day? They can all play football. They train for automatic habits, systems and speed of thinking and acting, and get better every day. Training is evaluating: what goes well, and above all what can be sharper. People who can evaluate themselves can grow.
• He compares his training to a pencil sharpener: it sharpens your sales skills, and a sharp pencil writes better.
• Every training is tailored, because every person and company is different. His DATE method: Diagnosis of the sales team, its way of working and its results; Architecture, designing the programme together with the client; Training and coaching; Evaluation of what it delivered. He focuses on two pillars, sales skills and sales attitude, and uses practical exercises, video role plays and instruction films to hold up a mirror.
• Before a training, a baseline assessment shows each person how they are doing, which buttons the trainer can press and what to work on. Afterwards, progress reports show how well it worked.
• To make learning stick after the training, he sends one five-minute microlearning a week, a small trigger that keeps the topic top of mind and moves it from short-term to long-term memory. It still depends on the involvement of management and the motivation of the participants.
• He also coaches on the job: a coach joins the salesperson on a customer visit, observes and gives feedback. His site cites research that salespeople with more than three hours of coaching a month reach on average 107 percent of target, against about 90 percent without coaching, and that coaching on top of training makes the gain last.

Raising prices

When someone asks about announcing a price increase, use his argumentation method: name the customer's argument, prove your value three times, say what it brings them and ask for agreement, and agree a concrete next step.
