WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• The sale happens in the buyer's mind. Colly says it does not happen in your presentation, your proposal or your closing technique. Before logic gets a seat at the table, the buyer's brain is asking two silent questions: is this safe, and can I trust this person? Until those are answered, no features, benefits or data will move the conversation.
• A brain-based sale follows a sequence. First reduce threat: your tone, your pace and your presence signal safety. Then build trust, not through persuasion but through understanding. Only when the emotional brain is settled does the rational brain engage with your ideas, numbers and solution. Many well-structured presentations fail because they are logically sound but mistimed: they speak to a brain that is not ready to listen. You stop trying to control the outcome and start guiding the process.
• Most sales training starts in the wrong place. Colly says we train people on products, services, presentations and processes, all important, but buying decisions are made in the human brain. Long before a prospect compares specifications, their brain is asking: do I trust this person, do they understand my situation, is this relevant to me, is there enough value to justify change, is the risk acceptable?
• Salespeople talk too early and listen too late. They present before they understand the buyer, so the buyer feels overloaded rather than understood. Objections turn into arguments and good opportunities stall, and nobody is quite sure why. Colly says this is not a talent problem, it is a structure problem. Most sales conversations go wrong in the first ten minutes, when the seller starts talking about the product before they understand the buyer's world.
• The BRAIN Selling System, five stages that follow the way a buyer thinks. Background comes first, because you need to understand their world before you say a word about yours. Results and Challenges shows what is working for them and what is not. Ambitions tells you where they want to be. Impact is where the buyer sees, in their own terms, what changes if they act and what it costs if they do not. Next Steps is a clear way forward that you agree together. He calls it a structure, not a script, so people put the questions into their own words.
• When a buyer pushes back, CALM keeps the conversation open: Cushion, Ask, Listen, Meet.
• Value is discovered, not declared. Buyers are rarely persuaded by what a salesperson tells them; they are persuaded by what they hear themselves say. The moment you declare value, the buyer's brain starts looking for reasons to doubt you, however sincere you are. People remember what they work out for themselves, value what they help to build, and resist conclusions pressed on them. Don't talk product features or price, because that only creates a gap between what you say and the value the buyer can see.
• We have no budget. Colly says those words rarely mean what they say. Budget follows conviction: organisations find money for what they believe in, so the buyer has probably not yet seen enough value to move money from somewhere else. Price only feels high in relation to the value the buyer can see. Before leaving the conversation ask three things: do you understand what the problem is costing them today, have they put a number on it in their own words, and can they see clearly what changes when it is solved? When the cost of doing nothing becomes larger than the cost of acting, budget has a way of appearing. Resist the urge to discount; go back in and find what standing still is costing them. The objection is rarely about money, it is about value not yet seen.
• Reframing objections. A frame gives context to how you see something, and changing the frame can change how you perceive and react to an experience. The purpose of reframing is to help a person see the situation from a different perspective and have more choice in how they respond.
• Your job is no longer to persuade. Colly says that in a world where buyers arrive already researched, the job is to reduce decision anxiety and be the steady, trustworthy voice that gives them clarity.
• Mindset. Colly says performance rarely breaks down because of capability, it breaks down because of mindset, meaning in the behavioural sense: how people interpret pressure, what their internal dialogue sounds like, and how quickly they return to clear thinking. Not positive thinking, but understanding how your brain works under pressure and using that.
• The power of yet. I'm not good at discovery shuts learning down; I'm not good at discovery yet keeps the door open. One word turns frustration into curiosity and struggle into feedback.
• Rejection. The best salespeople study it. Every no carries data about fit, timing and message, and that data is where real performance gains are made. It feels personal, and understanding why helps you regain control in the moment.
• Event plus response equals outcome. Colly asks how you respond to the events in your life, because the response decides the outcome, and he says all successful people take charge of their thinking. A winning mindset is something you build, and he describes the winning mindset as a wiring of the brain and body, not wishing.
• Applying Stephen Covey's seven habits to selling. Be proactive and take action, since nothing happens till we take action and excuses cost deals. Begin with the end in mind, so before a call or presentation know your objective. Put first things first: focus on the twenty percent of actions that produce eighty percent of results.
• From Brian Tracy, whom Colly credits with helping him build his first sales team in the early eighties: write your goal down, because a goal kept in your head is only a wish, and do the hardest task first.
• Artificial intelligence in sales. Colly says AI is essentially code, structured thinking at speed. Used as a system, it saves hours on prospecting, planning and research, and in a training session for one sales team the team produced fifteen qualified leads with a follow-up cadence in a single working block. But AI does not build trust, listen between the lines or read the emotional temperature in a room. With agents doing the research and the shortlisting, the conversation is the one part of the sale left to you, and it carries more weight now, not less. A human being still has to say yes, and that person decides with a brain that has not had an upgrade. AI can prepare you for the meeting, but it cannot make a buyer feel understood.
• Proposal drift. After a strong discovery call and a proposal, most stalled deals do not collapse, they lose momentum, and optimism replaces visibility. AI linked to the CRM can flag inactivity, trigger follow-up, surface at-risk deals and remove unrealistic close dates. Forecasting should be operational, not emotional.
• Sales culture. Colly says culture is neurology at scale: what a team feels walking in on a Monday morning shapes every conversation they have with buyers, and a leader's own state matters.
• Sales strategy. Align sales behaviours with the tasks the strategy needs, and check that salespeople spend their time and effort on the right activities, with the motivation and skills to sell on value.
• The four pillars of modern sales performance: trust, safety, discovery and value, as a connected system. Colly says the best salespeople are not the most persuasive, they are the ones who make buying feel safe. When a buyer's brain senses pressure, curiosity shuts down, risk tolerance shrinks and decisions stall, and no clever closing technique can override that. The old playbook of confidence, urgency and momentum works against you.
• The three brains in every sales conversation. The survival brain asks is this person safe, the emotional brain asks do I trust them, and the thinking brain asks does the logic make sense. Most presentations speak only to the thinking brain, but decisions are made in the first two, and the thinking brain only explains the decision the emotional brain has already made. He tells of two salespeople with the same product and market, one of whom broke most rules and still won nearly one hundred and forty percent of target, and says the difference was which part of the buyer's brain each was speaking to.
• Trust. The buyer decides in the first few minutes, probably seconds, whether you are safe enough to stay open to, and if you get that wrong they listen politely from behind glass. The brain trusts you when it feels understood, not when it feels impressed. He draws on Paul Zak's research on oxytocin and Charles Green's four factors of trust. Most sales meetings fail before the solution is discussed, because the salesperson presents value while the buyer's brain is still asking am I safe here. Polite interest in the room and no movement afterwards is the sign.
• Don't teach people to handle objections, prevent them. Colly says that when a prospect senses they are being handled, trust evaporates. Most objections are not commercial, they are neurological: the harder you push back, the more the resistance grows, and an objection is a signal that the buyer doesn't yet feel safe, clear or confident enough to move. Too expensive is often about perceived value or trust, we need to think about it is usually a comfort gap and not a calendar issue, we're happy with our current supplier is status quo bias. Something earlier in the conversation broke down: a need was not uncovered, a value was not connected, or a concern was glossed over to reach the close. So go back upstream, ask better questions, listen more deeply and earn the right to the next step.
• Objections come back wearing different clothes. A price concern dealt with too quickly returns as timing, and timing pushed aside returns as we need to speak to the board. The mistake is answering the surface before understanding the source, because every objection comes from one of three places in the buyer's brain and each asks for something different. Pre-empt the objections you know are coming, and reframe the ones that surface using CALM. When a buyer says I need to think about it, they don't need more logic, they need more certainty: slow down and rebuild clarity and don't push harder. When the buyer pushes back, lower the pressure.
• Stop presenting, start asking. Most salespeople lose the sale before they present because they begin with answers instead of questions. The quality of your questions determines the quality of your sales intelligence, and that decides the outcome. The seller who asks, leads: a good question uncovers the real need, reveals the cost of standing still, maps the buying group and lets the buyer describe value in their own words, the only version they truly believe. Discovery is a ritual that earns the right to sell, the point where you move from vendor to trusted advisor.
• Impact, the fourth stage of BRAIN. Most opportunities stall not because the buyer fails to see value but because they have not yet felt the cost of the problem. Colly describes six kinds of cost hiding inside doing nothing, four levels of impact from immediate to personal, and seven questions that surface the real weight of a problem. A buyer's brain treats a possible loss as heavier than an equal gain, and honest urgency uses that truth without ever manufacturing fear.
• An honest pipeline. Colly says most pipelines are not inaccurate, they are fiction: meetings get postponed, follow-ups go unanswered, proposals sink under twenty other priorities, and the deal stays in the system as active. The strongest sales leaders run fewer deals that are better qualified, with clear next steps, multiple stakeholders engaged and real urgency. Single-threaded deals, with one contact and one champion, drift when that person gets busy, so within fourteen days of first engagement you should know who influences the decision, who controls the budget and who carries operational responsibility. A forecast that depends on individual relationships is fragile.
• Clarity precedes velocity. Most businesses try to improve conversion before they improve clarity: they tweak messaging, pricing and training but cannot precisely describe their most profitable client. When the ideal customer profile is vague, prospecting is expensive; when it is sharp, conversations accelerate. He asks whether every person on your team could give the same one-sentence definition of your ideal customer.
• Prospecting is a discipline, not something you do when the pipeline looks thin. Protect a prospecting block like a meeting with your future revenue, target with precision because focus beats volume, craft messaging that creates resonance and not noise, and be consistent, since a steady daily rhythm compounds while random bursts create peaks and valleys. Rejection is part of the process, not a verdict on you: detach from the outcome, take the data, reset fast.
• Rejection and recovery. Colly says rejection hurts the brain, and in his book he offers a way to recover in ninety seconds. He calls the Resilience Reflex treating rejection as data, not a verdict, and says every no carries information about fit, timing and message.
• The six pillars of a winning sales mindset, which he says to read as one structure and not a list of tips: belief, resilience, optimism, growth, empathy and discipline. If all six feel weak, begin with one. The pillar you most want to avoid is usually the one costing you most. He also teaches five daily disciplines for sales success, the first being mental priming for three to five minutes, and says good intentions collapse by February because of three traps, overcommitment, environment and isolation, so you need an execution system that survives the real world.
• Smart calling and prospecting by phone, from his conversation with Art Sobczak. Colly says leave voicemails: if you leave fifty and get one reply it was worth it, since each takes fifteen or twenty seconds. For the person who answers the phone, he says make them your friend, then a good friend, then your best friend, and use their name back to them. He has used regular mail with great success to get in the door, for example a letter to a CEO who passed it on before Colly called.
• Stories in B2B. Facts inform, features explain, stories persuade. He teaches a five-part sales story framework.
• AI in sales. After fifty-six years he says AI will not replace great salespeople, but salespeople who use AI will replace those who don't, and he has watched fax, email and CRM split salespeople the same way into those who feared replacement and those who learned. AI handles identification and humans build trust: it clears the undergrowth so you reach the right conversation faster, but it will not find your prospects for you. In a sixty-second ChatGPT prompt he got ten named companies, their likely sales challenge and an opening conversation for each, which he calls a well-constructed brief and not magic. He calls the formula the AI plus human edge.
• Don't automate away the apprenticeship. A new salesperson can produce the work of a twenty-year veteran by Friday of the first week, but that does not make them one. If AI does the research, the writing and the preparation, where does a new salesperson learn to think? Judgement is built by working through material that resists. He also says don't automate a conclusion you cannot explain, and that AI maturity is not the number of tools you have deployed but your ability to explain decisions, improve performance and preserve human judgement. Too many AI tools can slow a sales team down.
• Six eras of selling. Colly started selling when there were no computers or CRM, with notebooks and telephone appointments, and he has watched the product era, the relationship era, the consultative era, the data-driven era and now the AI era. One truth has not changed: the deal is never won by the product, it is won inside the human mind. He started salesxcellence at fifty-two with a telephone and a notepad, thirty years ago.
