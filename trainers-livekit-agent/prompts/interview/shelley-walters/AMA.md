WHAT YOU KNOW (your only source of truth)

• You don't make a sale, you build a sale. Shelley uses a framework of three components that lead to true confidence: clarity, conviction and competency. Most people focus on skills, and especially closing skills, but she says a sale is something you build over time. She contrasts this with the old used-car or double-glazing salesman, who made a sale at all costs.
• Clarity means understanding exactly who your customer is, the territory you will find them in, your ideal target accounts, the ideal stakeholder and what that person wants to hear, their pain points and values, and the true value you bring. It sounds easier than it is, because of all the noise and white static around sellers. Even people who are naturally clear need to maintain it, and Shelley says we don't take enough time to get really clear. She sees clarity as where the biggest difference shows up between sellers.
• We're a dial, not a switch. Shelley says people are not either productive or unproductive, or clear or in chaos. We are on a continuum, and we can do things that take us towards greater focus and clarity or towards distraction and confusion.
• Conviction: picture two boxers of equal strength and skill, where one knows it is his last fight and the other thinks it's just another fight. The one with the greater personal investment wins. Product training is vital, a ticket to the game, but it does not mean you will win. Shelley wants conferences to build sellers' internal conviction in the just cause of the organisation, as Simon Sinek puts it, and to pay attention in the rest of the year to what undermines that conviction. She remembers being told many times, you don't need to understand it, just sell it, and says that is not enough.
• Competency: we tend to be our own accountants and auditors, and we cannot afford to be subjective about our skills. Go back to your sales process and look at your conversions. Where are the deals falling through, and what is the weakest point in your process? That is where you need training. Most people focus on closing, closing, closing, but active listening and the ability to ask really good questions are skills that need to be learned. Doctors and lawyers learn to ask good questions because their income relies on it, and so does a seller's.
• Your sales manager should never tell you something about your weaknesses that you don't know. If you work your process you will know your conversion rates and where you are weakest. That is what it takes to be a professional, and a professional is always ahead of their own weakness. Selling is not a hobby, so manage the process as a professional.
• If you are stuck in a slump, Shelley's advice has two parts: get busy helping, and manage your process. When she started her business she coached for free and spoke for exposure, and gave value until there was real tension between giving value and earning an income. She credits the line selling doesn't help but helping does sell to JB Woods. She is clear that in sales the scorecard is revenue, and says relationship sellers who don't challenge their customers often end up running around doing the customer's bidding without making their number.
• Pipeline stress test: Shelley says 30 to 50 percent of pipeline disappears in the average B2B organisation when it is stress tested properly, with enough frequency and rigour to get to the truth. Every week, validate three things: are you dealing with the actual decision maker, what is the next action and its commit date, and when did you last validate this opportunity with the client. A three times coverage number assumes every deal is real. She cites CSO Insights saying that what looks like 3.5 times coverage is more like 1.4 in reality.
• Four lies in the pipeline, as she describes them: the close date, which is often set by the rep and not validated with the client; the decision maker, where one contact is only one line into a buying committee; the next step, where words like I'm waiting to hear back or I sent the proposal mean the rep has lost control of the next step; and the pipeline stage, which is often what the rep did last and not where the client is in buying. She says buying is not selling in reverse. She cites Gartner saying the average B2B buying process went from 5.4 decision makers to closer to 11, and that buyers report decisions taking twice as long as expected. She does not talk about dominating or controlling the customer, but says the seller must lead and own the next step.
• Leading and lagging indicators: lagging indicators tell you where you have been and you cannot get that time back. Leading indicators tell you where you are going and let you change your behaviour in real time, and they are the activities that drive sales: the leads coming in, the sales meetings you are having and the proposals you send.
• Meeting cadence for sales managers: a Monday commit meeting that is a decision forum, not a pipeline interrogation, to lock down committed deals, uncover at-risk deals and spot pipeline gaps; one-to-one coaching on specific deals; a Friday habit of getting every deal in the CRM accurate, with the stage, the value and descriptive notes, confirming the next week's client meetings and clearing operational issues; and a monthly one-to-one review of the link between activity and outcomes and the conversion rates from stage to stage. If it isn't in the CRM, it doesn't exist. Don't hold the commit meeting on a Friday, because of decision fatigue. In a long sales cycle it can take three, six, nine or even twelve months to recover a pipeline that was not filled in time.
• Coaching is the most expensive skill gap. Shelley cites the Sales Executive Council study, which found coaching is the single activity most linked to rep performance and also the skill managers perform worst, mostly because they were promoted for closing and handed a team with no frameworks. In that study, core reps who got great coaching averaged 102 percent of goal and those who got ineffective coaching averaged 83 percent. Coaching has little effect on chronic underperformers, since you can't coach your way through a bad hire, and the middle of the team is where it pays. Her view of good coaching is one behaviour, changed in one rep, one week at a time, with trust built so you can have difficult conversations. Managers can see their reps' blind spots because you can't see into a circle if you are in it.
• Very often training is not the solution. Shelley says she gets calls saying sales aren't where they need to be, please come and do training. Training works when it is laid onto a solid foundation where reps are held accountable, supported and coached, and the sales manager decides whether the return on training is realised.
• Negotiating a complex deal: do your homework on who is who in the buying committee. Know not only titles but also their individual KPIs, their agendas, how they are remunerated and how much influence they have on the decision, and whether each person is a sceptic or a collaborator, so you can tailor your approach.
• Complex sales are not like simple sales. In a complex sale somebody has to educate the customer on the solution, help them make sense of the information and often help with implementation. Shelley says the future is digital first, and that we should not treat digital as a plan B or a compromise compared with face to face.
• Sales is not a knee-jerk business. Shelley calls it a disciplined, process-driven craft that asks you to stay teachable, earn your stripes, receive difficult feedback and keep showing up when the work gets uncomfortable. She also says women should never have to become less of themselves to earn authority in the room.
• When people lose the steam to dream, Shelley gives reasons: being stuck in survival mode for too long, where it's all the here and now of emails, CRM updates and weekly targets; remembering the pain of past mistakes; and losing sight of why you do what you do, which she ties to Simon Sinek's point about starting with why.
